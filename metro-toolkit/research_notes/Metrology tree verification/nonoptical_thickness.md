# Non-optical film thickness metrology: 4PP / eddy-current Rs, XRF, XRR, picosecond ultrasonics, XPS

Scope note: public sources only (vendor pages, application notes, NIST/ISO/ASTM/SEMI abstracts, peer-reviewed papers). Ranges are labelled "typical" and are vendor- or lab-reported unless stated. Researched 2026-10-17.

## Q1. Sheet resistance by four-point probe (4PP): principle, formula notation, range, contact/destructiveness, where it is used, throughput, limitations

### Takeaway
The learner's physics is right: R = ρL/A with A = W·t, Rs = ρ/t, so t = ρ/Rs, and for an in-line, equal-spacing probe on an infinite thin sheet Rs = (π/ln2)·V/I ≈ 4.532·V/I. Two claims need correcting. First, 4PP is a contact method, and its ~1 mm probe spacing and ~3 mm measurement area are far too large for scribe-lane pads, so it runs on blanket monitor wafers, not "on production wafers in SLAM". Second, it is not limited to films > 300 Å. It measures any conductive layer, including thin metals, implant layers and diffused layers. The "> 300 Å / optics not enough" reason applies to opaque metals only.

### Cited Findings
**Principle and formula**
- 4PP: current is forced through the two outer probes and voltage is measured across the two inner probes; Rs = CF·(V/I), and CF = 4.532 for probes far from the edge of the sample (semi-infinite slab). The same slide gives ρ = R·Area/l and Sheet Resistance = ρ/t — [NIST/Solid State Measurements, Brown 2005](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/pml/div683/conference/Brown-Benjamin_2005.pdf)
- KLA-Tencor OmniMapper (in-line needles ~1 mm apart): Rs = (π/ln2)·V/I, assuming infinitesimal tips and a sample that is semi-infinite laterally; resistivity = Rs × film thickness — [ASU Core Facilities, KLA Omnimapper](https://cores.research.asu.edu/semiconductor-device-processing/advanced-electronics-and-packaging/equipment-and-rates/kla-tencor-omnimapper-4-point-probe)
- Geometric correction factors are needed for finite, circular samples and for probes placed off-centre or near the edge (extensive tables) — [NBS Technical Note 199, Swartzendruber 1964](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/TN/nbstechnicalnote199.pdf); [NIST record](https://www.nist.gov/publications/correction-factor-tables-four-point-probe-resistivity-measurements-thin-circular)
- Standards: ASTM F84 / SEMI MF84 covers the **resistivity of silicon wafers** with an in-line 4PP. JIS H 0602 is the Japanese equivalent — [Napson measurement principles](https://www.napson-resistivity.com/technique). ASTM F1529 covers **sheet-resistance uniformity** using the dual-configuration in-line 4PP. Its range runs "from below 10 mΩ/□ for metal films to over 25 000 Ω/□ for thin silicon films", and it does not require probe-position or probe-spacing measurement (unlike F81, F84, F374) — [ASTM F1529-02](https://store.astm.org/f1529-02.html)
- The dual-configuration method is typically used where current crowds at a film edge or where pin spacing varies — [AZoM / KLA Instruments 2025](https://www.azom.com/article.aspx?ArticleID=24723)

**Range, precision, throughput (typical)**
- Typical 4PP range is 1E-3 to 1E+9 Ω/□ — [Napson](https://www.napson-resistivity.com/technique). The KLA Filmetrics R54 4PP spans a "ten-decade range" — [CN Tech / KLA R54](https://www.cntech.co.uk/p/r54-sheet-resistance-measurement)
- CDE ResMap specifications: repeatability (1σ) ±0.02% static and ±0.1% at nearby sites; accuracy < ±0.5%; about **1 s per site**; maximum throughput **40 wafers/h at 49 sites** (36 wph for one 300 mm configuration). The metal-film probe (Type A) has a 40 µm tip radius, 100 g force and 1.0 mm spacing. The manual notes that film thickness can be derived from Rs — [CDE ResMap manual (U. Arizona)](https://nfc.arizona.edu/sites/default/files/2022-02/cde_operation_manual.pdf)
- 4PP measurement area is about 3 mm (1.5–4.77 mm). Eddy-current measurement area is Φ5–60 mm — [Napson products](http://www.napson-resistivity.com/products/)
- Upper thickness limit: on very thick metal the voltage drop between the inner pins becomes very small, which limits 4PP to metal films no more than "a few microns" thick, depending on resistivity — [AZoM / KLA Filmetrics R50](https://www.azom.com/article.aspx?ArticleID=20588)
- KLA recommends 4PP for thinner metal films (> 10 Ω/□) and eddy current for thicker, highly conductive metals, and reports high 4PP/EC correlation — [CN Tech / KLA R54](https://www.cntech.co.uk/p/r54-sheet-resistance-measurement)

**Contact / destructiveness / where it is used**
- Napson lists the 4PP disadvantage as "Sample may be damaged by probe contact". The eddy-current advantages are "No damage by contact" and "No influence of contact resistance" — [Napson products](http://www.napson-resistivity.com/products/)
- On ultra-shallow implant layers, "particular precautions to reduce probe penetration" may be needed. An anneal is required before 4PP to make contact and activate the dopant, and the measurement "can be performed on the implant monitor wafer". Reliable down to ~1E12 ions/cm² — [AZoM / KLA Instruments 2025](https://www.azom.com/article.aspx?ArticleID=24723)
- Classic implant monitoring: "test wafers are implanted, annealed and the resulting sheet resistance is measured using a four point probe" — [Nucl. Instrum. Methods B, 1993](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0168583X9395055A)
- Running 4PP on small areas is a research modification. NIST developed a technique using probe arrays at standard 2×n probe-card dimensions (160 µm), combined with 3D current-flow simulation, because standard 4PP needs much larger film areas — [NIST, ICMTS 1999](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=5777)
- Applications listed by vendors: implant films, metal films, doped Si, epi layers — [CDE ResMap manual](https://nfc.arizona.edu/sites/default/files/2022-02/cde_operation_manual.pdf); metal-film uniformity (e.g., a TiWN batch-reactor fingerprint), implant and anneal characterization, and lamp or backside-contact problems after RTP — [CN Tech / KLA R54](https://www.cntech.co.uk/p/r54-sheet-resistance-measurement)

**Thickness from Rs needs a known (thickness-dependent) resistivity**
- NIST measured evaporated Cu films 10–150 nm thick: resistivity rises as the film gets thinner (surface and grain-boundary scattering). The electron mean free path in Cu at room temperature is 39 nm. Matthiessen's rule can still be used to infer thickness from Rs if the size-effect model is applied, and the results agreed within 3 nm of profilometry — [NIST, Schuster et al.](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=32595)
- Resistivity scaling in thin films and lines follows Fuchs–Sondheimer and Mayadas–Shatzkes (surface and grain-boundary scattering). Ru, Ir and Pt show a weaker thickness dependence than Cu, and below ~5 nm their resistivity is comparable to or lower than Cu — [Dutta et al., arXiv:1701.04124](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.04124)

**Why optics fail on metals (context for "> 300 Å where optics are not enough")**
- "Metals strongly absorb at all wavelengths, so the maximum layer thickness which can be determined [by ellipsometry] lays around 100 nm" — [Quantum Design ellipsometry intro](https://qd-europe.com/de/en/product/short-introduction-ellipsometry)
- In white-light interferometry, most films are no longer opaque below 50–100 nm — [Bruker AN-583](https://www.bruker.com/en/products-and-solutions/test-and-measurement/3d-optical-profilers/resource-library/an-583-thickness-measurements-of-opaque-and-transparent-films-or-coatings-with-wli.html)

### Inferences
- **Formula notation:** "ρ = R·A/L with A = W·t; Rs = ρ/t; t = ρ/Rs" is algebraically correct (it is R = ρL/A rearranged). Two caveats for learners: (1) for a square of film, R = Rs, which is why the unit is "Ω/□"; (2) t = ρ/Rs only holds if ρ is the **thin-film** resistivity at that thickness and microstructure, calibrated against a reference thickness method such as XRR, XRF or TEM. Bulk ρ underestimates thin-film ρ, so it will overestimate thickness for thin Cu, W and similar films. Also, Rs = 4.532·V/I assumes probe spacing s ≫ t, a sample much larger than s, and probes far from the edge; otherwise correction factors apply.
- **"> 300 Å where optics are not enough":** partly right. The optical limit for metals is roughly where the film becomes opaque, quoted as ~50–100 nm in the sources above. On many metals ellipsometry already loses thickness sensitivity at a few tens of nm. So 300 Å (30 nm) is a reasonable rule-of-thumb lower bound for when "optics are not enough", but it is material-dependent and not a lower limit of 4PP (4PP works on thin metal and implant layers). Practical upper limit for 4PP: a few µm of metal (signal too small). Eddy current is preferred for thick, low-Rs metal.
- **"Destructive; measured on production wafers in SLAM":** CORRECTED. 4PP is a contact, quasi-destructive technique (probe marks, possible penetration of ultra-thin layers, and possible particles). Fabs run it on blanket monitor or test wafers (implant monitors after anneal, metal deposition monitors). A ~1 mm-spaced probe needing a ~3 mm area cannot land in a scribe-lane metrology pad. On product wafers, sheet resistance is obtained electrically from scribe-line test structures (van der Pauw/Kelvin) at parametric (WAT/PCM) test. That is a separate electrical-test node, not inline 4PP. The "SLAM on product wafers" attribute fits picosecond ultrasonics and micro-spot XRF better, and may have been carried over by mistake in the learner's tree.
- **"Not integrated, fast ~2 s, typically 49 points":** consistent with the sources (49-site maps; ~1 s/site plus moves ≈ 1–2 s/site; standalone tools with SECS/GEM). Treat it as VERIFIED (typical).

### Gaps
- KLA OmniMap RS-100/RS-200 spec pages were blocked (KLA site returns "Access Denied"), so current KLA RS-series throughput and repeatability figures could not be quoted.
- No public source found defining the acronym "SLAM (Scribe Lane Area Mark)". It may be fab-specific jargon.
- No public source found giving typical scribe-lane metrology pad dimensions, to compare quantitatively against 4PP probe spacing.

## Q2. Non-contact eddy-current sheet resistance (the common alternative to 4PP)

### Takeaway
An AC coil induces eddy currents in the film and the tool measures the conductance (electrical loss). It is non-contact and non-destructive, but needs calibration against references and a gap/lift-off correction. It works best for thick, highly conductive metals (Rs ≲ 10 Ω/□, ≳ 100 nm), has a large spot (mm to cm), and is the basis of in-situ Cu-CMP endpoint and thickness sensors.

### Cited Findings
- An AC coil induces eddy currents; the tool measures electrical loss. The signal depends on sheet resistance (thin layers) or resistivity × thickness (wafers), and on the coil-to-sample distance. Advantages: non-destructive, non-contact, "no wearing parts unlike 4PP". Semilab LEI-1510 range is 0.035–3200 Ω/□ in three ranges — [Semilab LEI-1510](https://semilab.com/category/products/non-contact-sheet-resistance-eddy-current)
- An eddy-current tool directly measures conductance. Sheet resistance is calculated from it, and resistivity also needs the specimen thickness. SEMI MF673 covers the set-up and calibration of non-contact eddy-current gauges — [SEMI MF673](https://store-us.semi.org/products/mf067300-semi-mf673-test-method-for-measuring-resistivity-of-semiconductor-wafers-or-sheet-resistance-of-semiconductor-films-with-a-noncontact-eddy-current-gauge)
- Eddy-current disadvantage: "Calibration to a reference resistivity sample is required". Measurement area Φ5–60 mm, probe gap 0.1–10 mm — [Napson products](http://www.napson-resistivity.com/products/)
- Lower limit: eddy current is restricted to about ≥ 100 nm of metal (≈ 10 Ω/□, metal-dependent) because very thin films generate small eddy currents. This is partly due to the small R50-EC spot — [AZoM / KLA Filmetrics R50](https://www.azom.com/article.aspx?ArticleID=20588)
- A capacitive distance sensor is often co-located so true Rs can be computed from both the eddy signal and the gap — [Semilab TCO sheet resistance](https://semilab.com/category/products/tco-sheet-resistance)
- In-situ Cu-CMP thickness monitoring uses optimized eddy-current transducers; combined optical + eddy-current systems cover Cu from 1500 nm down to < 30 nm — [Qu et al., Microelectron. Eng. 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167931713002633)

### Inferences
- In a training tree, eddy current should sit next to 4PP as the "non-contact Rs" option. It suits thick Cu/Al/W and is usable on product wafers in principle, but its mm-scale spot averages over patterns, so blanket monitors remain typical inline. Thickness still comes from t = ρ/Rs, so the same resistivity caveat applies.

### Gaps
- No current public spec found for fab-grade eddy-current Rs mapping tools (e.g., KLA RS-series EC options) giving repeatability in %; KLA pages were blocked.

## Q3. XRF (X-ray fluorescence) for metal film thickness

### Takeaway
XRF measures the characteristic X-ray intensity of the film element, or the attenuation of the substrate line. That gives mass per unit area, which becomes thickness through an assumed density. It is non-contact and non-destructive, and works on blanket wafers and, with micro-spot optics plus pattern recognition, on product-wafer pads. It needs calibration standards or fundamental-parameter (FP) models. "Too thick" most plausibly means the saturation limit: as the film approaches its saturation (information) depth, intensity stops changing with thickness and sensitivity collapses. "10 Å–50 kÅ" is a plausible typical span, but it is element- and line-dependent.

### Cited Findings
- XRF determines coating **mass per unit area** (and linear thickness if the density is known). Three modes: emission (coating line rises with thickness), absorption (substrate line falls) and ratio. **Saturation thickness** is the "thickness that, if exceeded, does not produce any detectable change in fluorescent intensity", and it depends on the line energy. The most accurate range is a normalized intensity of about 0.3–0.8. Calibration requires standards of known mass per unit area, with density documented — [ISO 3497:2000 summary](https://standards.iteh.ai/catalog/standards/iso/653c536e-966d-40e2-b6e0-1cc23505a968/iso-3497-2000); [EN ISO 3497 definitions](https://standards.iteh.ai/catalog/standards/cen/0c4e66f0-0aa0-4625-955f-db7de4695bad/en-iso-3497-2000)
- XRF measures from a few atomic layers up to the saturation thickness, "typically in the range of 6 to 60 µm" for metal coatings, depending on density, average Z and line energy. Both substrate and coating produce signals, so calibration approach matters — [Thermo Fisher, XRF coating thickness](https://documents.thermofisher.com/TFS-Assets/CAD/Reference-Materials/qlt-article-xrf-thickness.pdf)
- Example "infinite thickness" values: Au Lα in gold ≈ 11 µm; Ni Kα in steel ≈ 45 µm; the matrix's effective Z sets attenuation (a matrix effect) — [VRXRF explainer](https://www.vrxrf.com/blog/infinite-thickness-xrf-accurate-elemental-analysis-coatings-pellets) (secondary/commercial source; consistent with ISO and Thermo)
- Au-L intensity saturates as gold approaches infinite thickness. Below ~500 nm the intensity–thickness relation is linear — [Bruker M6 Jetstream app note](https://www.bruker.com/en/products-and-solutions/elemental-analyzers/micro-xrf-spectrometers/m6-jetstream/metal-coating-layer-thickness-cultural-heritage.html)
- Rigaku Cu seed note: "Saturation in XRF is a specific depth of the layer whereby intensity no longer changes beyond this layer thickness". For 1 µm Cu, the top layer is "much below the saturation depth". Cu thickness is measured by a **direct method (0–0.7 µm, Cu Lα, linear)** and an **indirect method (0.7–1.4 µm, substrate Si Kα attenuation, exponential)** — [Rigaku RSMD-20101936](https://rigaku.com/products/semiconductor-metrology/application-notes/rsmd-20101936-cu-seed-and-thickness)
- Ultrathin end: 3 nm TiN with "sensitivity to thickness variation of down to 1 Å"; 3 nm Co repeatability shown — [Rigaku RSMD-20101835](https://rigaku.com/products/semiconductor-metrology/application-notes/rsmd-20101835-ultrathin-film-thickness). Ru: precision < 0.2 Å for Ru ≥ 1 Å (60 s acquisition; 9 sites in < 13 min) — [Rigaku RSMD-20101831](https://rigaku.com/products/semiconductor-metrology/application-notes/rsmd-20101831-ultrathin-ru-thickness-measurement-combined-xrd)
- Product-wafer capability: Rigaku ONYX 3000 does in-line measurements on "blanket and product wafers", using micro-spot polycapillary optics and pattern-recognition position control — [Rigaku ONYX 3000](https://rigaku.com/products/semiconductor-metrology/wdxrf/ppc-na-onyx-3000); [ASM International 2020](https://www.asminternational.org/results/-/journal_content/56/10192/41410226/NEWS). XTRAIA MF-3000-R spot sizes are φ20–85 µm FWHM — [Rigaku MF-3000-R](https://rigaku.com/products/semiconductor-metrology/xrr-edxrf-and-optical-tools/xtraia-mf-3000-r). MF-3000 covers "thicknesses from Ångstroms to microns" on blanket and product wafers — [Rigaku MF-3000](https://rigaku.com/products/semiconductor-metrology/xrr-edxrf-and-optical-tools/xtraia-mf-3000?setLang=english)
- WDXRF fab tools (Rigaku WaferX 310, 4 kW Rh tube) are **blanket-wafer** metrology for BPSG/PSG, metal films and WSix, with simultaneous thickness and composition — [Rigaku WaferX 310](https://rigaku.com/products/semiconductor-metrology/wdxrf/waferx-310). The Malvern Panalytical 2830 ZT WDXRF runs up to 25 wafers/h, with light elements down to boron — [Malvern Panalytical](https://www.malvernpanalytical.com/en/industries/electronics/silicon-semiconductors)
- XRR is often used to characterize the thickness standards for XRF calibration — [Rigaku XRR technique](https://rigaku.com/resources/techniques/x-ray-reflectometry-xrr)
- FP quantification can be improved with standards; it is used for metallization on wafers — [Bruker film thickness](https://www.bruker.com/it/applications/academia-materials-science/thin-films-and-coatings/film-thickness-determination.html)
- In-line XRF is co-acquired during in-line XPS (Nova VeraFlex) to quantify buried SiGe beyond the XPS depth limit — [Nova SPIE 2026](https://www.novami.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/139810a-xps-integrated-trgts.pdf)
- Jordan Valley (now Bruker) small-spot XRF and XRR applications: high-k/metal gate, Cu seed/barrier, Cu ECP and CMP, UBM, Sn/Ag micro-bumps, Cu pillars — [PR Newswire 2011](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/jordan-valley-delivers-a-multiple-system-repeat-order-for-its-thin-films-metrology-systems-from-a-major-foundry-123986879.html)

### Inferences
- **"10 Å ~ 50 kÅ" (1 nm–5 µm): VERIFIED as a typical, element-dependent range.** The lower end is conservative: sub-Å precision on Ru ≥ 1 Å has been shown. The upper end is set by saturation and loss of slope. For soft lines (e.g., Cu Lα) the direct method already ends near 0.7 µm, and the Rigaku note extends Cu to ~1.4 µm by switching to substrate attenuation. Harder K-lines extend to many µm (ISO/Thermo: saturation 6–60 µm). So 5 µm is achievable for some metals and lines but not universal.
- **"Too thick" (as written) — most likely meaning:** the XRF limitation for thick films. Near saturation the intensity–thickness curve flattens (ISO's best range is normalized intensity 0.3–0.8), so dI/dt → 0 and precision degrades until thickness becomes unmeasurable. An alternative reading is a tree-routing note ("use XRF when the film is too thick for XRR"). That is also physically consistent, since XRR stops resolving fringes above ~200–500 nm. The trainer should state both and pick the intended meaning.
- Further limitations to teach: XRF gives mass per area, so thickness needs an assumed density (density changes from porosity or phase are misread as thickness). It needs calibration standards or FP models. Matrix and secondary-fluorescence effects arise in multilayers. Signals overlap when the same element appears in several layers (e.g., Ti in Ti/TiN; Ta in Ta/TaN), and XRF alone cannot separate TiN from Ti. It cannot distinguish a thin dense film from a thicker porous one. Light elements need vacuum/WDXRF.
- Throughput is typically slower than 4PP (seconds to tens of seconds acquisition per site; e.g., 60 s/site in the Ru example; ≤ 25 wph for WDXRF).

### Gaps
- No single public source gives a vendor-stated "10 Å–50 kÅ" spec line. The range is inferred from the combined application notes and standards.
- No public quantitative data found on XRF throughput per site for current 300 mm in-line micro-spot tools (Bruker Sirius-RF, Nova VeraFlex XRF channel).

## Q4. XRR (X-ray reflectivity) for thickness and density

### Takeaway
XRR measures specular X-ray reflectivity at grazing angles. The critical angle gives electron density (hence mass density), the Kiessig fringe period gives thickness, and the fringe damping gives surface and interface roughness. It is non-contact, non-destructive and first-principles (no calibration), and often serves as the reference for XRF. A typical measurable range is ~1 nm to ~200 nm, with some sources quoting up to ~500 nm and one up to 1 µm. The learner's "< 500 Å" (50 nm) is too conservative as a general limit; it applies only to low-resolution set-ups or heavy, rough films. "Too rough" is correct as a limitation: roughness above ~3–5 nm damps the fringes and the specular signal.

### Cited Findings
- Total reflection occurs below the critical angle. Thickness comes from the oscillation period, and surface/interface information from the angle dependence of the amplitude. Typical range is "~1 nm to several hundred nanometers". XRR distinguishes stack layers with ≥ ~5% density difference, is "a first-principles (absolute) technique that requires no calibration", and "is often used to characterize thickness standards for XRF" — [Rigaku XRR technique](https://rigaku.com/resources/techniques/x-ray-reflectometry-xrr)
- Layer thickness 1–500 nm; surface/interface roughness < 3–5 nm required; density from the critical-angle position; Kiessig fringe spacing Δθ gives t ≈ λ/(2Δθ) — [Iowa State thin-film characterization lecture](https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/canfield/files/inline-files/Characterizations%20Films%20class%202.pdf)
- "Due to the roughness, the detectable layer thickness range is around 2–200 nm". Accuracy ~0.2 nm in thickness; needs very smooth substrates (glass, Si); density determined without weighing — [Fraunhofer IST](https://www.ist.fraunhofer.de/en/expertise/analytics-testing-technology/xrr.html)
- Malvern Panalytical Aeris XRR: 1–200 nm — [Malvern Aeris XRR](https://www2.malvernpanalytical.com/Aeris/X-ray-reflectometry)
- LLNL: surface/interface roughness of "merely 2 nm can adversely affect" spectra (PuO2/Pu). Because of instrumental resolution, their set-up was "ideal only for thicknesses of less than 50 nm" — [LLNL/OSTI 1236734](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1236734)
- Portable XRR: surface roughness < ~4 nm required to determine thickness and density reliably — [Imashuku, X-Ray Spectrometry 2025](https://analyticalsciencejournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/xrs.3499?af=R)
- XRR applicable for films 1 nm–1 µm, sensitive to surface roughness — [Lishchenko et al. 2026, J. Nanotechnol.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/jnt/8821511)
- In-fab micro-spot XRR/XRF/XRD (Rigaku XTRAIA MF-3000-R): spot φ20–85 µm FWHM; multilayer analysis up to 200 nm total film thickness; layer-by-layer density mapping — [Rigaku MF-3000-R](https://rigaku.com/products/semiconductor-metrology/xrr-edxrf-and-optical-tools/xtraia-mf-3000-r)
- Rapid (parallel-detection) XRR for semiconductor fabs: thickness, density and roughness measured without standards; percent-scale thickness accuracy confirmed on Ti, TiN, TiN-on-Ti and Ta2O5 against XRF and RBS — [Koppel & Parobek, OSTI 21202317](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/21202317)
- Rigaku XRR mapping: an Au film (target 50 nm) measured 38.5 nm at centre, rising to 43.5 nm at the edge; thicker films give a shorter fringe period — [Rigaku B-XRD2030](https://rigaku.com/hubfs/2024%20Rigaku%20Global%20Site/Resource%20Hub/Applications%20Library/PDF/145539861880.pdf?hsLang=en)
- Bruker in-line XRR platforms: Sirius-RF (µXRF + XRR), JVX7300LSI (HRXRD + XRR, large and small spot, blanket and patterned wafers, high-k/metal gate); XRR and HRXRD are "first principle techniques that deliver accurate and precise results without calibration" — [Bruker press release 2016](https://ir.bruker.com/press-releases/press-release-details/2016/Leading-Logic-and-Foundry-Manufacturers-Select-Bruker-X-Ray-Metrology-Tools/default.aspx); [Bruker X-ray metrology](https://www.bruker.com/en/products-and-solutions/semiconductor-solutions/automated-x-ray-metrology-systems.html)

### Inferences
- **"XRR range < 500 Å": CORRECTED.** The typical lab/fab range is ~1 nm to ~200 nm (some sources: up to 500 nm–1 µm with high-resolution optics). In-fab micro-spot tools quote ≤ 200 nm total stack. The upper limit comes from fringe resolution: the fringe period Δθ ≈ λ/(2t) shrinks as t grows until the instrument's angular resolution and beam divergence can no longer resolve it. Strongly absorbing heavy-metal films and roughness reduce it further. "< 500 Å" is a safe value for a sweet spot of high precision on ultra-thin films (high-k, barriers, seeds), not a hard limit.
- **"Too rough" (as written) — meaning:** XRR's main failure mode. Interface and surface roughness σ damps the specular reflectivity (roughly as a Gaussian factor in q·σ) and washes out the Kiessig fringes at higher angles. Above ~3–5 nm RMS roughness (some set-ups ~2 nm) thickness, density and roughness can no longer be fitted reliably. Rough polycrystalline thick metals (e.g., thick Cu, W, AlCu) are therefore poor XRR candidates, and XRF or picosecond ultrasonics are preferred there.
- **"Also measures density": VERIFIED.** Density comes from the critical angle (electron density). Layers need ≥ ~5% density contrast to be separated.
- **"Non-contact, non-destructive; blanket wafers and SLAM on product wafers": VERIFIED** for modern micro-spot tools with pattern recognition, with one caveat. At grazing incidence the beam footprint along the beam direction is much larger than the nominal spot (it scales as spot/sin θ), so pads must be long enough or software must correct for spill-over. This is an inference from geometry; no source quantifies the footprint.
- Other limitations: XRR needs a model (layer count, starting guesses) and fits can be non-unique; layers with similar density are hard to separate; acquisition is slower than optical methods.

### Gaps
- No public numeric spec found for in-fab XRR thickness precision (e.g., 3σ in Å) on current Bruker/Rigaku/Nova tools.
- No source found quantifying the footprint elongation for specific fab XRR tools on scribe-line pads.

## Q5. Picosecond ultrasonics (picosecond laser acoustics; Onto Innovation PULSE / MetaPULSE / Echo)

### Takeaway
A sub-ps pump pulse launches a strain (acoustic) pulse into an opaque film. A delayed probe pulse senses the echoes returning from interfaces, and thickness = sound velocity × round-trip time / 2. The method is non-contact and non-destructive, with a ~10 µm spot and pattern recognition, so it measures on-product pads. It can resolve up to ~6 layers at once with sub-Å repeatability (vendor claim). Range is ~40–50 Å to several µm (5 µm historically; up to 20–35 µm on newer tools). It requires an opaque or absorbing top layer (or a modified approach for transparent films) and a known or co-measured sound velocity. The learner's node is largely VERIFIED. Two corrections: the range extends well beyond "Å-scale", and the tool is not restricted to opaque films.

### Cited Findings
- Principle: a 0.1–0.2 ps green (515 nm) pump pulse is focused to a µm-scale spot to launch an acoustic wave. The round-trip time of echoes from interfaces is measured with a delayed probe pulse, and thickness is extracted from first principles using the speed of sound — [Onto, Advanced Packaging article](https://ontoinnovation.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Advanced-Interconnect-Process-Control-with-Picosecond-Ultrasonic-Technology-for-AI-Device-Packaging.pdf); [Onto, 3D NAND paper](https://ontoinnovation.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Monitoring-process-steps-in-3D-NAND-using-picosecond-ultrasonics.pdf); [PMC 2022 MEMS piezo study (MetaPULSE G, 515 nm, 60 MHz rep rate)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9693109)
- Worked example: echo time 12.9 ps and TaN sound velocity 53.6 Å/ps give TaN = 345.7 Å. The arithmetic implies t = v·Δt/2, with Δt the round trip — [Stoner, AIP Conf. Proc. 2001](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/acp/article-pdf/550/1/468/11602897/468_1_online.pdf)
- Range, accuracy and throughput (Rudolph 10-K): single or multilayer opaque films "from less than 40 Angstroms to greater than five microns"; "up to 70 wafers per hour"; "one to two percent accuracy and typically less than one percent repeatability"; up to six layers measured simultaneously on product wafers, non-contact — [Rudolph Technologies 10-K, FY2016 (SEC)](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1094392/000109439217000009/rtec10k12312016.htm)
- MetaPULSE 200: up to six layers; "Angstrom accuracy and sub-Angstrom repeatability at up to 60 wafers per hour"; a **10 µm spot** placed within existing metrology sites by pattern recognition; also adhesion, interlayer reaction, RMS roughness and silicide phase; reduces monitor-wafer use — [SemiconductorOnline, MetaPULSE 200](https://www.semiconductoronline.com/doc/metapulse-200-0001)
- MetaPULSE-II: 40 Å–5 µm with "Angstrom accuracy and sub-Angstrom repeatability"; site sizes down to 30 × 30 µm; post-CMP Cu thickness profiling of dense arrays (erosion control) — [Rudolph MetaPULSE-II datasheet](https://www.artisantg.com/info/Rudolph_Technologies_A22706_Datasheet_2019315162244.pdf)
- Onto Echo (current generation): in-line, on-product; thickness range **50 Å to 35 µm**; full-wafer mapping with 0.5 mm edge exclusion; applications include gate metals, plug/contact, barrier/seed, top metal, RF electrodes/IDT, UBM/RDL, 3D NAND hard mask, low-k modulus, **implant monitoring** and thermal conductivity — [Onto Echo](https://ontoinnovation.com/products/echo)
- 3D NAND: PULSE is the "process of record" for metal thickness (AlCu, W, Cu, Cu seed, Ta, TaN, Ti, TiN, WSi). It measures films "down to 50 Å and ~20 µm depending on the material's property". On semi-transparent amorphous carbon, thickness and velocity are both measured with 3σ repeatability < 0.5% (49-point maps, 0.5 mm EE), and accuracy correlates with cross-section SEM at R² > 0.95 — [Onto, 3D NAND VLSI paper](https://ontoinnovation.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Monitoring-process-steps-in-3D-NAND-using-picosecond-ultrasonics.pdf)
- Spot ~8 × 10 µm²; RDL and bond-pad metal — [Onto AP article](https://ontoinnovation.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Advanced-Interconnect-Process-Control-with-Picosecond-Ultrasonic-Technology-for-AI-Device-Packaging.pdf). Backside metallization layers 50 nm–3 µm (stacks to 5 µm); spot on the order of microns allows pads as small as 15 µm for in-line monitoring on product wafers — [Onto 2026 BSM paper](https://ontoinnovation.com/resources/resource-advanced-metrology-for-backside-metallization-using-picosecond-laser-ultrasonics/)
- Transparent films: refractive index, sound velocity and thickness of transparent films can be obtained from multi-angle picosecond ultrasonics (research method) — [Devos et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/234872125_Refractive_index_sound_velocity_and_thickness_of_thin_transparent_films_from_multiple_angles_picosecond_ultrasonics)
- Semilab also sells an opto-acoustic (surface-wave, impulsive stimulated) thin-metal thickness system for process wafers; it compares the measured oscillation to known material properties — [Semilab surface acoustic wave](https://semilab.com/category/products/surface-acoustic-wave-measurement)

### Inferences
- **"Precision < 1 Å": VERIFIED (vendor claim: "sub-Angstrom repeatability")** for typical thin metals. For thick films, repeatability is better expressed as a fraction: < 1% (10-K), < 0.5% 3σ (a-C), so a 1 µm film has repeatability ~ several Å to tens of Å.
- **"Opaque films, Å-scale thickness": CORRECTED/expanded.** The core use is opaque (metal) films, but the range spans ~40–50 Å to ≥ 5 µm (up to 20–35 µm on Echo), not just Å-scale. Semi-transparent films (a-C hard mask) and low-k modulus are also measured. "Å-scale" should read "Å-level precision".
- **"Non-destructive; mostly on SLAM; blanket or product wafers": VERIFIED.** The 10 µm spot fits ≥ 15–30 µm pads, with pattern recognition. It is also used for post-CMP dense arrays, as well as blanket monitors.
- **"Not integrated": plausible.** All sources describe standalone in-line tools; no integrated (in-process-tool) version was found.
- **"Fast 2–5 s": UNRESOLVED but consistent.** Throughput is 60–70 wph (for an unstated site count); no public per-site time was found.
- Limitations to teach: (1) sound velocity must be known (it depends on phase, density and stress) or co-measured; errors in velocity scale directly into thickness. (2) The top layer must absorb the pump, so transparent films need special approaches. (3) Very thin films (< ~40–50 Å) give echoes too close to the excitation transient. (4) Acoustic impedance contrast between layers is needed to see interfaces. (5) Multi-layer fits need a stack model.

### Gaps
- No public per-measurement time (seconds/site) found for MetaPULSE/Echo.
- Current Echo repeatability specs (Å, 3σ) by film type are not public beyond older "sub-Å" claims.

## Q6. XPS used for film thickness (thin overlayers via effective attenuation length)

### Takeaway
XPS thickness uses the exponential attenuation of photoelectrons. Substrate intensity falls as I = I∞·exp(−d/(EAL·cos α)) and overlayer intensity rises correspondingly, so d follows from intensity ratios, using an EAL from NIST databases. It is highly precise below ~2 nm and loses sensitivity beyond ~10 nm with Al Kα. That matches "thickness < 100 Å" and "information depth ≈ 3×EAL ≲ 10 nm". In-line XPS (Nova VeraFlex) has been used since 2004 for gate stacks, with a ~50 µm spot on pads or integrated targets. The learner's node is VERIFIED.

### Cited Findings
- Overlayer thickness is the most common EAL use. Substrate intensity decreases exponentially and overlayer intensity increases exponentially with thickness, and the equations are rearranged for thickness by replacing the IMFP with the thickness EAL (L_TH). The EAL differs from the IMFP because of elastic scattering, and depends on configuration and thickness. Average EALs are valid for emission angles 0–50° near the magic angle (ψ = 55°), and predictive formulas reproduced Monte Carlo thicknesses within 1.6–2.4%. The **information depth** is the "maximum depth ... from which useful information is obtained", identified with the thickness from which a specified percentage (e.g., 95% or 99%) of the signal originates. IMFPs for < 1 keV are ~0.4–2 nm. Island growth violates the uniform-film assumption — [NIST, Powell & Jablonski practical guide (JVST A 2020)](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=929257)
- The NIST EAL database supplies practical EALs for overlayer-thickness measurements; a single average value is usable for a range of thicknesses and emission angles up to ~60° — [NIST, Powell & Jablonski 2005](https://www.nist.gov/publications/characterization-thin-films-nanometer-scale-auger-electron-spectroscopy-and-x-ray)
- Ignoring elastic scattering (using the IMFP instead of the EAL) biases SiO2-on-Si XPS thickness — [NIST, Powell & Jablonski 2001](https://www.nist.gov/publications/measurement-silicon-dioxide-film-thicknesses-x-ray-photoelectron-spectroscopy)
- Information depth of XPS is < 10 nm. The Si 2p attenuation length with Al Kα is about 2.8 nm. ARXPS gives thickness and composition non-destructively to about 10 nm — [Thermo Fisher ARXPS app note](https://documents.thermofisher.com/TFS-Assets/CAD/Application-Notes/D16069~.pdf); [Thermo Fisher ARXPS page](https://www.thermofisher.com/us/en/home/materials-science/learning-center/surface-analysis/angle-resolved-xps.html)
- Nova (SPIE 2026): in-line XPS was introduced in 2004 for the 65 nm node, when ellipsometry hit accuracy limits on gate stacks. The VeraFlex IV uses Al Kα in a **50 µm spot** on 300 mm wafers and runs in vacuum < 5×10⁻⁷ Torr with a load-lock queue. I_Si = I_Si∞·exp(−d/EAL) scaling is used for an SiO overlayer. XPS gives "precise detection of small changes in ultrathin films < 2 nm", while bulk scattering causes "effective loss of measurement sensitivity beyond > 10 nm" with Al Kα. Each layer needs a unique peak. Measurements are mostly on blanket wafers or blanket pads, increasingly on integrated targets (S/D epi Ge%, post-CMP residues, GAA nanosheet high-k/Si). Co-acquired XRF extends to buried SiGe — [Nova SPIE 13981 (2026)](https://www.novami.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/139810a-xps-integrated-trgts.pdf)
- Nova positions XPS as simultaneous thickness and composition "without correlation", with direct atom quantification and chemical-state identification — [Nova XPS technology](https://www.novami.com/nova-technology/x-ray-photoelectron-spectroscopy-xps); [Nova materials metrology brochure](https://www.novami.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/nova-brochure-materials-metrology-2022-compressed-1.pdf)

### Inferences
- **"Thickness < 100 Å via EAL": VERIFIED.** If elastic scattering is neglected, the fraction of signal from depth ≤ z is 1 − exp(−z/(λ cos α)), so 95% comes from z ≈ 3λ cos α (ln 20 ≈ 3.0). With λ ≈ 2–3 nm for typical Al Kα core lines, 3λ ≈ 6–9 nm, so ≲ 10 nm. The practical measurable overlayer thickness is ≲ 8–10 nm (≈ 80–100 Å), with the best precision below ~2–5 nm.
- XPS is non-destructive in angle-resolved/fixed-angle mode, unlike sputter depth profiling. In-line tools are standalone, in vacuum, slower than optical methods, and typically measure blanket pads ≥ 50 µm.
- Typical in-fab thickness applications: high-k (HfO2) and interfacial SiO2/SiON, nitrogen dose in SiON, TiN/TaN metal-gate and work-function layers, ultrathin barriers and liners, SiGe Ge%. The learner's composition node is the right home; the thickness node can cross-link.

### Gaps
- No public numeric precision (Å, 3σ) found for Nova VeraFlex thickness measurements.
- No public per-site measurement time for in-line XPS found.

### Inferences
- Suggested technique-chooser logic for the toolkit (inference from the above):
  - **Conductive blanket monitor, need Rs or uniformity map:** 4PP (thin/medium films, implant after anneal) or eddy current (thick, low-Rs metal, non-contact).
  - **Opaque metal on product pads, single layer or multilayer, Å precision:** picosecond ultrasonics.
  - **Elemental mass/thickness and composition, ultrathin to µm, product pads:** micro-spot XRF (watch saturation and same-element overlaps).
  - **Ultrathin smooth films needing absolute thickness plus density and roughness, or a reference for XRF:** XRR (≤ ~200 nm, roughness < ~3–5 nm).
  - **Sub-10 nm films needing thickness plus chemistry (high-k, SiON N-dose, TiN):** in-line XPS.
- Typical application mapping: Cu seed/barrier (Ta/TaN, TiN, Co, Ru liners) → PU, XRF, XRR (thin) and XPS (ultrathin); thick Cu/AlCu/W → PU, eddy current, 4PP; silicides (NiSi, CoSi2, WSix) → 4PP Rs on monitors (phase-sensitive), PU (phase detection) and WDXRF (WSix composition); implant → 4PP after anneal (PU-based implant monitoring is also offered on Echo).

- Fab-internal practices (which steps use which tool, sampling plans) are not public; mappings above are typical, from vendor application lists.
- Vendor specifications for Nova, Bruker and KLA current-generation tools (precision in Å, throughput) are largely not public; KLA pages were inaccessible during this research.
